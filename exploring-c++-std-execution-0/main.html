This post is the first entry in a series of posts dissecting C++ the framework for asynchronous computation proposed for standardization: std::execution. The main purpose of the series is to develop an understanding of std::execution's API and the rationale behind it from first principles – or at least my understanding of it.

Computation and continuations

We begin with the bare basics of the concepts behind std::execution: computations and continuations.

A computation can be intuitively understood as a process by which some inputs are converted into some outputs by executing a sequence of steps in order1. At any given time, the execution state of a computation is composed of two kinds of steps: those that have already been executed and those to be executed. The steps to be executed can be further subdivided into the next immediate step to be executed and the remaining (non-immediate) steps to be executed. The former step is known as the reducible expression (or redex) and the latter steps conform the continuation.

Example 1: Arithmetic expressions

Given the expression 1 + 2 * 3, the usual precedence rules tell us that the first step to be executed is 2 * 3. That is, 2 * 3 is the redex and 1 + ... is the continuation.

While 2 * 3 is executed, the result of 1 + ... remains undetermined. We can think of 1 + ... as being suspended until the result of 2 * 3 is available.

Once the result is available, we substitute the ellipsis in 1 + ... with it. This substitution process is commonly known as applying the continuation. At this point there is one single step to be executed and an empty continuation (no non-immediate next steps). In other words, after executing the redex, the computation is done (see Figure below, where the resolved 2 * 3 in the redex is signaled with a different font color).

This silly example already shows a key property of continuations: they are comprised not solely of operational descriptions (+), but also of the data needed to execute these operations once the continuation is applied (1).

Example 2: Subsequent statements

There is no reason to limit the abstraction level of "steps" just to arithmetic expressions. We could take a step to be a statement. The order of execution is given by the usual top-to-bottom statement execution.

Consider 

1: auto sqx = x * x;
2: auto sqy = y * y;
3: auto r = sqx + sqy;

A valid execution state has the statement in line 2 as its redex and statements of lines 3 as its continuation.

Disregarding practicality, it does not really matter how granular or abstract a step is taken to be when describing a computation, we can still categorize the parts of the execution state.

Example 3: Function boundaries

So far none of the examples have function calls in them, but this does not have to be the case. Given the function hypotenuse as defined below 

1: double hypotenuse(double x, double y) {
2:   auto sqx = sq(x);
3:   auto sqy = sq(y);
4:   auto r = add(sqx, sqy);
5:   return sqrt(r);
6: }

If the execution state has sq(x) as its redex, then its continuation is conformed by the assignment to sqx, statements of lines 5, 6 and 7, and whatever the caller of hypotenuse does after hypotenuse returns.

Unlike the preceding examples where steps were always at the same abstraction level (either all steps were arithmetic expressions or statements), here we have characterized the steps of a computation using a mixture of function call expressions, assignments, statements, and whatever the caller executes after returning from hypotenuse. So not only can we take the steps that characterize a computation to be as coarse or fine grained as we want – as shown by Example 2 –, but we can also mix and match abstraction levels when describing a single computation.

Regardless of the abstraction level at which we decide to operate in, the continuations can always be thought of as "whatever remains to be executed", and the redex as "whatever is immediately executed".

The next example shows some more revelant properties of continuations and programs.

Example 4: Conditional branches

Consider the ternary expression rand() % 2 ? foo() : bar(). What is the continuation when the redex is rand() % 2. Is it necessary to know the result of rand() % 2 to determine the continuation?

This is a tricky question. There is no such thing as the continuation at this point in the execution, but rather two (!) continuations (as depicted above). After evaluating the conditional, one continuation is discarded and the other one is applied.

This example, short as it is, illustrates three more properties of continuations: (1) there is not necessarily one continuation at any given time; (2) continuations do not have to be applied, they can be discarded; and (3) a continuation may be applied or discarded based on runtime information.

Next steps

In this post we have settled some basics of continutions and their properties via examples. The continutaions we have dealt with, however, are not very interesting in the sense that we cannot do anything with them. Throughout the next few posts we will explore the Continuation Passing Style, from which we will develop a basic form of std::execution.
